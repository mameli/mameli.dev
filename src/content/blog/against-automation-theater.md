---
title: 'Against Automation Theater'
description: 'Why I am not convinced by the race toward total automation and why I prefer small, personal tools that are actually useful.'
pubDate: '2026-03-23'
---

I do not fully understand all the hype I keep seeing around, especially on Twitter, about people building automations with tools like OpenClaw or Claude Code to read thousands of emails, search for articles across dozens of websites, and stay on top of everything, only to never really go deep on any of it.

I also see kanban boards used only to assign tasks to sub-agents, as if all the value lived in the complexity of the setup rather than in what it actually gives you in the end. From an engineering point of view, these may be fun and interesting experiments, but I am not convinced they are all that useful in real life.

## The appeal of total automation

I like exploring these tools too. It would be hypocritical to pretend otherwise. They have enormous potential, and they are among the most interesting things to experiment with right now.

My issue is that I think people are overdoing it. Too often I see people chasing an idea of total automation that feels compelling mostly because it is new. If an agent reads for you everything you were never going to read, filters everything you were never going to study in depth, and organizes work you may not have needed to do in the first place, the risk is that you build a very sophisticated machine that only moves the problem a few meters away instead of solving it.

## The problem is not only technical

There is another aspect that worries me much more: privacy and security.

Many people with no technical background at all are starting to experiment with these tools without fully realizing how risky they can be. Giving access to your email, browser, repositories, calendars, credentials, or other accounts to tools that are still maturing is not a trivial decision.

There have already been multiple stories about compromised accounts, exposed data, and lost credentials, and situations like these can genuinely ruin your life. That is why I find it hard to share the general enthusiasm when I see some of these workflows presented as harmless or ready for everyone.

## The incentive problem

What also stands out to me is how these tools are almost always framed: more productivity, more outreach, more marketing, more content, more optimization, more money.

Some people use them to automate email, self-promotion, and other forms of spam disguised as efficiency. And I have the impression that part of the public conversation around AI automation is driven by exactly this: not by the real value of the tools, but by their ability to generate attention.

Without getting into conspiracy theories about crypto bros or hidden agendas, I do think there is a real incentive problem here. Too often the question is not "does this help anyone?" but "does this let me do more?".

## And yet the tool can be useful

This is exactly where I think it is worth pausing for a moment, because the tool itself can be genuinely useful.

Advances in artificial intelligence have made speech-to-text almost trivial, and for many people with disabilities that is a huge blessing. The same goes for many tools designed for blind and visually impaired users, or for anyone who needs more accessible interfaces and practical support in daily life.

These are real, simple examples, and much more interesting than many demos built only to impress. They remind us that artificial intelligence can truly improve people's lives when it is used to remove friction, restore autonomy, or lighten a real burden.

## The use case that interests me

That is the point I want to make in this article.

I am not interested in the race to produce more and more, or in the idea of optimizing every single aspect of my day. I am much more interested in using these technologies as tools that can help you in life in a concrete, almost ordinary way.

The kind of AI automation I find interesting usually has nothing special about it from an engineering perspective. It is not a technical masterpiece, not a futuristic framework, and it does not need a dashboard full of agents talking to each other. If it is genuinely useful in everyday life, that already feels like a win to me.

It helps me in life in the same way a calendar helps me by reminding me about events I need to follow. I do not need it to feel more productive or to fill the internet with more slop. I need it to remove a small mental burden and leave a little more space for the things I actually care about.

## Not everything has to become turbo-capitalism

It makes me sad to see that, for many people, all of this ends up becoming just another way to get richer, do more, and squeeze every available minute. It is a very American worldview, and I would not want it to become the global paradigm through which we look at AI.

There is no need to turn everything into turbo-capitalism. Sometimes it is enough to build something that works for us, actually helps us, and does not produce more useless complexity.

Maybe the real point is not to ask how many things we can automate, but which burdens are actually worth removing. If a small automation can do that, then it already has more value than many much louder experiments.
